About the episode

Sanjay Aggarwal is a Venture Partner at F Prime Capital and the author of the 2026 State of Robotics Report, one of the most widely circulated annual snapshots of the global robotics ecosystem. In this Physical AI Series episode he walks through what the numbers in that report actually mean.

The headline is that robotics investment is at an all-time high. Underneath it, the money is concentrating hard: roughly 70 to 80 percent of dollars are going into rounds above $100–200M, and only 40 to 50 companies a year raise at that scale. Some of that reflects genuine scale-up: Waymo’s $16B round is a business growing fast. Some of it is a bet that the LLM playbook can be replayed in the physical world.

From there the conversation gets specific about what works. Why the humanoid hardware is maturing faster than the software. Why most useful robots are not humanoids at all. Why warehousing matured before agriculture. And why India has world-class robotics talent but not yet the capital layer to keep frontier companies at home.

  • The conversation covers:
  • The mega round economy: close to $20B invested across the US and Europe in each of the last two years, most of it concentrated in a handful of names.
  • The two parallel paths in humanoids: hardware that has matured quickly, software that has not.
  • Why most useful robots are purpose-built: if a single task repeats, the humanoid is rarely the right form factor.
  • The four-part framework for why robotics companies fail: market, use case, technology, capital strategy.
  • Why material movement on the farm worked while crop harvesting stayed out of reach.
  • Autonomous vehicles moving from a technology bet to a scale-up bet, and what that unlocks downstream in trucking, sidewalk delivery and AMRs.
  • Why the US leads on software and China on hardware, adoption speed and a lower IPO bar, and why the two are becoming parallel markets rather than competing ones.
  • India in robotics: strong technical talent, a still-nascent deep tech VC ecosystem, and why billion-dollar frontier rounds only really happen in the US.
