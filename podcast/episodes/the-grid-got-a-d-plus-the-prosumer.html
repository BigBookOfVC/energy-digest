About the episode

Gareth Evans is the founder and CEO of VECKTA, a software platform and marketplace that helps commercial and industrial businesses deploy on-site energy systems. He started as an environmental scientist cleaning up old oil and gas well sites, which took him to Iraq at the end of the second Gulf War; watching a population sitting on the world’s largest reserves survive on two hours of power a day is where the thesis began.

The framing that runs through the episode is a correction. The common view is that the energy problem is one of generation. Gareth’s argument is that generation costs have fallen while delivery costs have risen, and that the grid carrying power from central plants across thousands of miles of transmission line is the part that is failing. The American Society of Civil Engineers has downgraded it from a C-minus to a D-plus.

What follows from that is the prosumer shift: businesses generating, storing and trading their own power rather than consuming someone else’s. The back half turns to venture, where Gareth walks through three rounds raised across the hardest years in climate tech, including the single word in his deck that was quietly shrinking how investors sized his market.

  • The conversation covers:
  • Why the US grid earned a D-plus, and what it means to build a business on infrastructure that is forty to a hundred years old.
  • The $150B that US businesses lose to outages annually, against commercial power costs up 22% and industrial up 30% in four years.
  • Power delivery, not generation, as the real bottleneck: generation got cheaper while transmission got more expensive.
  • Why small, agile systems at the edge beat mega-projects on permitting, cost and speed, and why SMRs have stayed ten years away for an entire career.
  • The prosumer model: on-site generation, storage, automated trading with neighbours, and monetised emission-reduction credits.
  • Why generalist VCs are re-entering energy after years away, and the friction that creates around deal cycles and business models.
  • The one word in a pitch deck that shrank investors’ view of the total addressable market.
  • The K2 lesson: why climbers die clinging to their ice axes, and what that says about tools versus culture.
