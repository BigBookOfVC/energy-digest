About the episode

Ashish Goel has been building for eighteen or nineteen years. His first venture was in books, publishing and kids’ media, sold in 2011. He then co-founded Urban Ladder, ran it as CEO, and sold it to the Reliance Group in 2020. The thread back to energy runs further than that: a gas engine and gas turbine system he helped design as a consultant in 2006 still sits under Cyber Hub in Gurugram, and he ran a clean energy blog from 2009 to 2011, then spent years angel investing in the sector before starting Optimist with Pranav, a mechanical engineer from IIT Madras with thermal sciences and an industrial master’s from Cambridge.

The argument at the centre of the episode is that the air conditioner answers a question nobody in India asked. It was invented as a dehumidification device and then refined by American, European, Japanese and Korean firms for temperate climates. There are three variables of thermal comfort, he says, and the machine gets all three wrong here: it throws air colder than it needs to, drier than anyone wants, and slower than an Indian body prefers. The reason it cannot be fixed at the surface is what he calls a wheels within wheels problem. The output temperature is set by the evaporator, the evaporator is set by the need to condense water, and that need traces back to a preference for low humidity in countries that are not this one. Changing the answer means redesigning the components, the firmware and the control algorithm, not the thermostat.

Then comes the number this episode will be quoted for. Twenty-five to twenty-eight per cent of India’s annual peak load, which lands in the third or fourth week of May between four and six in the afternoon, comes from room air conditioners alone. On current assumptions that share reaches somewhere between thirty-eight and forty-five per cent by the late 2030s. And the peak is not one number: it breaks into millions of neighbourhood transformers, each sized for a local load and none of them upgradeable. That, rather than national generation, is where he thinks the system breaks.

The rest is a second-time founder being unusually plain about operating. Why a new brand in a high-trust, high-ticket category launches online only. Why service, not product, dominates every review in this category, and why he thinks customers are fair rather than demanding. What carried over from Urban Ladder, which turns out to be values, foundational systems and doing obvious things consistently. And on capital, a cap table of about $12 million with Accel as largest institutional shareholder, plus a clear-eyed read on why Series B and C money for Indian hard tech follows breakout companies rather than leading them.
