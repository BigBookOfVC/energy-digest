About the episode

Pranav Pai is a founding partner and CIO of 3one4 Capital, an early stage firm in Bangalore just past its tenth year. He is an engineer by training, took his master’s at Stanford, was employee number three at a SaaS company that sold for just under $400 million, and came back to build the firm he thought was missing: Indian capital backing Indian innovation at the point where a company is still two or three people with a laptop. The funds run around $225 million, back 30 to 35 companies each, and about seventy per cent of those cheques are the first money in. Of a 36 person team, nine sit on the investment side; the rest work with companies after the wire, on finance, governance, revenue and capital markets access.

The number that frames the conversation is deal flow. 3one4 saw a thousand companies in its first year and close to ten thousand last year, compounding above thirty per cent a year. He is more interested in that curve than in any one fund’s returns, because it is the cleanest available measure of how fast the country is producing things worth funding, and because at that volume the top five hundred are genuinely good.

On the charge that Indian VCs are risk off, he argues both sides rather than defending his own. Founders, he says, forget the power they hold: their job is to put a vision against an investor’s cash and pressure to compound, and anyone who marries the two raises money anywhere in the world. Then he gives the other side without flinching. The 2009 to 2015 vintage had almost no domestic LP base, five or six year fund tenures, few Series Bs and no IPOs, and it was competing for money against a bank stock that compounded at 23 per cent for thirty years. That stock, not another fund, is the real competition for Indian venture.

The deep tech passage is the one to send people. India built a talent surplus without a grant surplus, a capital surplus or a demand surplus: three decades of foreign R&D centres trained generations of engineers here while the work was filed under back office. What changed is procurement. The government became a customer, which is what NASA was to SpaceX, and that is a different thing from handing out grants. On energy he is careful about what venture can actually hold. An enzyme that turns crop waste into ethanol feedstock, yes, and a long list of second and third order fixes besides. Another solar module line or another battery plant, no; those belong on a corporate balance sheet.

  • The contrarian view hiding in plain sight: 10,000 chemical factories fighting over local demand instead of selling globally.
