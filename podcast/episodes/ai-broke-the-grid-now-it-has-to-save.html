About the episode

Sean Kelly spent eleven years running power plants and trading electricity across Texas and the eastern US before founding Amperon in 2018, with AI and machine learning as the thesis from the first model rather than a later addition. The company is remote-first, under a hundred people, and forecasts for 165 customers and 51 million meters.

His framing is that forecasting quietly changed job. It began as a 15-day trading tool built on the ECMWF weather model, and everyone really only cared about the day ahead. Now the European model runs 46 days and even 13 months out, weather volatility shows up weeks in advance, and a one-in-100-year event arrives roughly every three months. Winter Storm Uri, Winter Storm Fern, the Iberian blackout. Forecasting stopped being a trading input and became grid-critical infrastructure.

The most useful analogy in the conversation is historical. Sean argues that what is happening with data centres now is what happened with demand response in the early 2000s: pay large industrial load to be on good behaviour when told. The bottling plant that swore it could never pause did pause. He expects compute to follow, and thinks the projects clearing interconnection queues will be the ones that promise flexibility.

  • The conversation covers:
  • Why AI has to win as the grid’s stabiliser over the next five years, because the alternative is bad reliability and bad prices.
  • How forecasting evolved from a 15-day trading tool into infrastructure, as the European model stretched to 46 days and 13 months.
  • Why one-in-100-year weather events now arrive every few months, and what that does to a forecast.
  • Why no single forecast wins: six to eight models under every number, automatically reweighted and retrained each hour.
  • Flexibility as the only real alternative to blackouts, and the 50 to 100 hours a year that actually matter.
  • Data centres as demand response 2.0, echoing the early-2000s playbook of paying large load to behave.
  • What is actually lagging: bureaucracy, phantom projects clogging interconnection queues, data, and talent.
  • Why there will never be one global grid, and why local market expertise becomes the moat.
  • Who captures the value between hyperscalers with compute, utilities guarding their data, and incumbents who cannot hire.
