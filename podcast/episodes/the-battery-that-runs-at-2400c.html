About the episode

Arvin Ganesan has sat in three different seats on the energy transition: the regulator, in senior roles at the US Environmental Protection Agency; the buyer, as head of global energy and environmental policy at Apple; and now the operator, as CEO of Fourth Power.

He opens by naming three structural changes that broke a grid which had run on flat demand for thirty or forty years: load growth from data centres but also heat pumps, EVs and industrial electrification; a generation mix that is far more diverse in its dispatch curves; and the physical effects of climate change on how the system operates.

Fourth Power’s answer is a thermal battery. Electricity heats tin, which radiates light onto carbon blocks that reach 2,400°C and hold that heat for days behind graphite insulation. To discharge, a thermophotovoltaic panel sits eight inches from the heat source and converts the light back to electricity within seconds. The company name comes from the physics: emitted light rises with the fourth power of temperature. Ninety-eight percent of the system is carbon, sourced from petroleum coke, which is what makes the supply chain argument the centre of the episode.

  • The conversation covers:
  • The three structural forces that changed how the grid operates compared to thirty years ago.
  • Why the short versus long duration debate misses the real question, which is what value the asset provides to the grid.
  • How the thermal battery stores energy in carbon at extreme temperature and discharges through a thermophotovoltaic panel.
  • Building from abundant materials: every country with a petroleum refinery produces the feedstock.
  • Why supply chain has become another word for scalability, and a primary signal for investors and customers.
  • Hyperscalers and utilities as two durable customer segments running on very different clocks.
  • Why long duration storage is a hedge against natural gas exposure, in the US and outside it.
  • The first commercial project as the hardest capital leap in deep tech hardware, and the discipline of retiring technology risk before scaling manufacturing.
