About the episode

Anand Jain brings twenty-five years to this: economics at Delhi School of Economics, six years in Mumbai across asset management and bank treasury, an MBA at Yale that delivered him onto Wall Street in 2008, then two years in Barclays Capital’s energy practice where he did his first solar and wind deals. He gave inputs into Gujarat’s solar policy, India’s first, from the United States, and when the Chief Minister asked what he was still doing there he came home, built a 10 MW project under that policy in 2009, set up KPMG’s solar practice, and deployed roughly a billion dollars in utility scale over the following decade. Aerem is five years old.

The question that started it is a good one. An Indian MSME pays 8 to 12 rupees a unit and a commercial user 12 to 18, while rooftop solar costs around 3 to 3.5. Payback lands between six months and three years, and then the power is effectively free for twenty-five. So why, when rooftop is 60 to 70 per cent of installed capacity globally, was it 15 per cent in India? Not policy, in his telling: net metering is standardised across most states with deemed approval after a fortnight, and the pushback he describes comes from the tier-two DISCOM employee losing a high-paying customer rather than from the statute.

His answer is that lending alone is shooting in the dark. A solar plant takes two to three months to execute, and a bank doing a credit check has no view of whether the installer is building it properly. So Aerem went full stack: an in-house NBFC, B2B commerce, and a solar engineering team running 100-plus quality checkpoints during execution, with monitoring afterwards that feeds plant performance history straight back into underwriting the next loan from the same EPC. Term loans are collateral free, secured only on the financed asset. About ₹600 crore disbursed, ₹1,000 crore of annual commerce sales, close to 5,000 installers, 3,500 plants and 1,500 MW.

Two passages are worth the time on their own. The first is why he built deliberately where there is no subsidy: subsidies put discretion in a few hands and do not produce high quality outcomes, and the MSME case stands on savings from day one without them. The second is his read on US residential solar finance, where savings were sold on subsidies and rising power prices that never materialised, and loan tenors stretched far past the five-year books he writes against five-year borrowings. India, he argues, is a different asset entirely: a third of the installation cost and roughly half again the radiation.

  • The conversation covers:
  • Why the savings case for Indian MSMEs is 30 to 80 per cent off the power bill, and why that still was not enough on its own.
  • How rooftop went from 15 per cent of installed capacity to around 25, and where he thinks it lands.
  • Why rooftop obviates transmission and distribution infrastructure entirely, even at a 15 to 20 per cent cost premium.
  • The deliberate decision to build where there is no subsidy, and what subsidies do to market quality.
  • Why installing a plant costs three to four times more elsewhere, and what higher radiation does to the comparison.
  • Why lending alone is shooting in the dark when execution takes two to three months.
  • How 100 plus quality checkpoints during execution feed directly back into underwriting.
  • Why term loans are collateral free, with the financed asset as the only security.
  • What went wrong in US residential solar: savings that were missold, and tenor mismatches.
  • Why only 3 to 4 per cent of the MSME opportunity has been solarised, in a segment he sizes at $500 billion.
