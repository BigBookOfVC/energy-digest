About the episode

Garima Bharadwaj is co-founder and CTO of Enlite Research, which she started eight years ago after watching e-commerce, fintech and D2C get rebuilt while real estate’s idea of technology stayed at listing aggregation. She had seen the operating side at OYO. A commercial building runs perhaps twenty disparate asset types and hundreds of independent machines, and the layer governing them was designed roughly thirty years ago by a handful of Western incumbents: binary controls and predefined logic. Enlite’s premise is an alternative stack for controlling the machines rather than the building.

The sharpest passage is on why that logic fails. A consultant sets it the day the building is made: run from 8am to 9pm, hold 24 degrees. Then three things move. The load, because occupancy is never the same two days running. The seasonality, because it rains for four hours and the city changes. And the machines themselves, whose parameters drift as they age. The logic sees none of it, because it is bound locally to a piece of hardware with no view of outdoor ambience, dynamic load or the grid. So it holds 24 degrees and draws more energy each year to do it. Her conclusion is that controls belong around individual machines, not a building envelope: a chiller plant and an air handling unit are different problems, and in most buildings the cooling plant has never once spoken to the occupancy system or the solar.

The company spent three and a half years building before selling anything, and six years bootstrapped in total. The traditional approach runs 40 to 50 kilometres of cable even for half a million square feet and ends in a desktop application reporting what already happened; they went cloud native and wireless, which meant convincing a notoriously slow industry to accept both at once. The first customer took 27 meetings over seven months. Two customers in year one became 5 million square feet in year two and 50 million in year three, with 70 customers, 12,000 controllers and roughly 3 billion parameters a day. They expect to pass 100 million square feet by March 2027.

On commercial model she is deliberately unfashionable. They refuse to lead with guaranteed savings, because performance contracting collapses into an argument about whether the technology or the site staff produced the number. They sell automation and uptime, as the incumbents do, and let the saving arrive as a by-product; at one Blackstone office park that meant complaints down 90 to 95 per cent, the BMS team from six people to one, and 15 to 17 per cent off the energy bill. The last stretch is about where this goes: buildings as responsive grid assets rather than passive loads, and why building codes, which buy good materials and good machines, cannot govern how those machines behave every day.
