About the episode

Dong-Su Kim is CEO and a board member of LG Technology Ventures, the corporate venture arm of the LG group. He starts by widening the frame: calling this a once-in-a-century transformation is not a statement about EVs. Homes are adopting heat pumps, induction cooktops and rooftop solar. Factories are moving off fossil fuels. Data centres are building their own storage. Humans have pushed civilisation forward by burning things since the Bronze Age; electricity arrived roughly two centuries ago and everything has been slowly moving onto it since.

On chemistry he is unromantic. Lithium ion or a variant of it stays dominant. LG is investing heavily in LFP for storage, because heavier and lower capacity matters less than cheaper, more stable and safer. Sodium is the one to watch next. Solid state and lithium metal have moved slower than expected, partly because customers turned out to care more about cost, reliability and safety than about capacity.

The most useful passage is on failure. Asked why so many well funded battery startups stalled, he does not reach for a single cause. A battery is a multi-variable optimisation, and manufacturing at yield is brutally hard even for an incumbent. He offers LG’s own Poland factory as the example, which is a more honest answer than most investors would give.

  • The conversation covers:
  • Why the transformation spans generation, storage, distribution and consumption rather than just mobility.
  • The virtuous cycle: AI raises demand for storage while also accelerating battery R&D and manufacturing optimisation.
  • Why the capital crunch in energy is temporary, and already starting to reverse as demand becomes visible.
  • Why 2035 is defined by coexistence: distribution limits mean even fusion would not remove the need for local generation.
  • Why lithium ion variants stay dominant, why LFP wins for storage, and why sodium is next.
  • Why customers now prioritise cost, reliability and safety over capacity, and what that did to solid state.
  • Why batteries are extremely hard to manufacture at scale with high yields, told through LG’s own factory.
  • Why China keeps its lead in refining and precursors, and why that is as much regulatory as technical.
  • Where startups can still win: mining, defence and space, and niches where a small player can go vertical.
  • The next frontier: storage moving into homes, appliances and vehicles over the next decade.
